<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Turn every percent into a multiplier. A 15% increase means multiply by 1.15, a 15% decrease means multiply by 0.85, and back-to-back changes multiply together. Percent change is always (new − old) ÷ old, and to undo a percent change you divide by the multiplier.</p></div>

Percentage questions show up across the SAT Math test, in word problems about prices, populations, survey results, and data tables. They belong to the Problem-Solving and Data Analysis domain, and they're some of the most learnable points on the test, because nearly all of them yield to one idea: a percent is a multiplier.

This guide works through the five kinds of SAT percentage problems, from the one-step "percent of" question to the reverse-percent problems that catch most students. Every number below was checked by code.

## The multiplier idea

Write every percent change as a single number you multiply by:

With multipliers, a two-step problem becomes one multiplication, and a reverse problem becomes one division. You'll see this in every example below.

## Type 1: Percent of a number

**What is 35% of 440?**

35% of 440 = 0.35 × 440 = 154.

The traps here are decimal slips: 1540 (moving the decimal the wrong way) and 286, which is 65% of 440, the part that's left over. If a question asks what remains after removing 35%, then 286 is right, so read carefully.

A useful related form: **12 is what percent of 48?** Divide the part by the whole: 12 ÷ 48 = 0.25, so 25%.

## Type 2: Percent change

**The price of an item changed from $180 to $198. By what percent did the price increase?**

Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100 = (198 − 180) ÷ 180 × 100 = 18 ÷ 180 × 100 = 10%.

The answer is 10%. The trap answer is about 9.1%, which comes from dividing by the new price, $198, instead of the original. Percent change is always measured against the starting value. Another trap is 18%, which treats the $18 change as if it were already a percent.

## Type 3: Back-to-back percent changes

**A store raises the price of a jacket by 10%. Later, it lowers the new price by 25%. Compared with the original price, what is the overall change?**

Multiply the two multipliers: 1.10 × 0.75 = 0.825. A multiplier of 0.825 means the final price is 82.5% of the original, a **decrease of 17.5%**.

If you'd rather use numbers, start with $100: after a 10% increase it's $110, and 25% off $110 is $110 × 0.75 = $82.50. Compared with $100, that's 17.5% less.

The trap is adding the percents: 10% − 25% = −15%. That's wrong because the 25% decrease applies to a bigger number than the 10% increase did.

Here's a version that surprises almost everyone: **raise a price by 25%, then lower it by 20%.** The multipliers are 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00, so there's **no change at all**. Adding the percents would give a 5% increase.

## Type 4: Reverse percents (finding the original)

**After a 20% discount, a pair of headphones costs $64. What was the original price?**

A 20% discount means the sale price is 0.80 times the original. So 0.80 × original = 64, and original = 64 ÷ 0.80 = $80.

The trap is to take 20% of $64 and add it back: 64 + 12.80 = $76.80. That fails for the same reason as Type 3: the 20% was taken from the original price, not from $64. Undo a percent change by dividing by its multiplier, never by applying the opposite percent.

Check your answer by running it forward: 20% off $80 is $80 × 0.80 = $64. ✓

## Type 5: Repeated growth

**A town's population is 2,000 and grows by 10% each year. What will the population be after 2 years?**

Each year multiplies by 1.10, so after 2 years: 2,000 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 2,000 × 1.21 = 2,420.

The trap is 2,400, which adds 10% of 2,000 twice. Growth by a percent compounds: the second year's 10% is taken from 2,200, not from 2,000. Written as a function, this is P(t) = 2,000(1.10)^t, which is exactly the kind of exponential model the SAT also tests in Advanced Math. See the [nonlinear functions practice](/sat/math/nonlinear-functions/) for more of those.

## Percent versus percentage points

When a question compares two percents, read the wording. If a rate goes from 20% to 25%, it rose by **5 percentage points**, but by **25 percent** (because 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25). The SAT uses both phrases on purpose, and the answer choices usually include both numbers.

## A checklist for any percent question

1. **What's the starting value?** Percent change and discounts are measured from it.
2. **Write the multiplier.** Increase: 1 + rate. Decrease: 1 − rate.
3. **Multiple changes?** Multiply the multipliers. Never add the percents.
4. **Finding the original?** Divide by the multiplier.
5. **Check the question.** Does it ask for the new amount, the change, or the percent?

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you calculate percent change on the SAT?

Subtract the old value from the new value, divide by the old value, and multiply by 100. A change from 180 to 198 is (198 − 180) ÷ 180 × 100 = 10%.

### Why can't I add two percent changes together?

Because the second change applies to a different amount than the first. A 10% increase followed by a 25% decrease gives 1.10 × 0.75 = 0.825, which is a 17.5% decrease, not 15%.

### How do I find the original price after a discount?

Divide the sale price by the multiplier. If $64 is the price after a 20% discount, the original is 64 ÷ 0.80 = $80.

### Are percent questions on the SAT calculator questions?

You can use a calculator on every SAT Math question. Setting up the right multiplier is the hard part; the arithmetic is quick either way.

### What domain do SAT percentage questions belong to?

Percentages are part of Problem-Solving and Data Analysis, one of the four Math domains, along with ratios, rates, data, and probability.
