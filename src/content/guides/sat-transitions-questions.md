<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Ignore the answer choices at first. Read the sentence before the blank and the sentence after it, then name their relationship in one word: contrast, addition, cause and effect, example, sequence, or clarification. Pick the transition from that group. Choices from the same group can't both be right, so the answer is the one whose meaning is different from the others.</p></div>

Transitions questions are some of the most learnable points in the SAT Reading and Writing section. Each one gives you a short passage with a blank between two ideas and four transition words or phrases. Your job is to choose the one that shows the logical relationship between the ideas. You don't need to know the topic. You need to know what each transition word means, and to read the two sentences around the blank carefully.

These questions belong to the Expression of Ideas domain. Below is a method that works on every SAT transitions question, a table of transition words grouped by meaning, and worked examples.

## The relationship test

1. **Cover the choices.**
2. **Read the sentence before the blank and the sentence after it.** Those two are usually all you need.
3. **Ask how the second idea relates to the first.** Does it contradict it? Add to it? Result from it? Give an example of it? Come later in time? Restate it?
4. **Name the relationship, then choose.** Only now look at the choices and find the word that matches.

The order matters. Students who read the choices first often pick the one that "sounds smart" instead of the one that fits the logic.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: contrast.**

> The earliest telescopes were strong enough to show that something unusual surrounded Saturn. ______, their blurry images made the rings look like two odd bumps on either side of the planet rather than a ring.
>
> A) Therefore<br>B) For instance<br>C) However<br>D) Similarly

**Answer: C.** The first sentence says the telescopes could detect something around Saturn; the second says the images were too blurry to show what it really was. That's a limitation of the first idea: contrast. "Therefore" would mean the blurry images *resulted* from being able to see the rings, which doesn't make sense.

**Example 2: cause and effect.**

> The bakery switched to a supplier that delivers every morning instead of twice a week. ______, it now throws away far less spoiled flour and fruit.
>
> A) As a result<br>B) Nevertheless<br>C) For example<br>D) Meanwhile

**Answer: A.** Daily deliveries lead to less spoiled stock, so the second sentence is a result of the first. "Nevertheless" would signal a surprise, but less waste is exactly what you'd expect.

**Example 3: example.**

> Some city parks are designed to do more than provide green space. ______, the riverside park in the town's center doubles as a flood basin, holding rainwater during heavy storms.
>
> A) In contrast<br>B) Consequently<br>C) For instance<br>D) Finally

**Answer: C.** The second sentence gives a specific case of the general claim in the first. Whenever the first sentence is general and the second is a specific instance, look for an example transition.

**Example 4: addition versus contrast.**

> The new bus route cut the average commute by ten minutes. ______, it connected two neighborhoods that previously had no direct service.
>
> A) However<br>B) In addition<br>C) Thus<br>D) Instead

**Answer: B.** Both sentences describe benefits of the route, so the second adds to the first. "However" would require the second sentence to work against the first.

## The traps the SAT uses most

**Same-meaning choices.** If two choices mean the same thing, such as "therefore" and "consequently," neither can be the only correct answer. Eliminate both and choose from what's left. This trick alone solves many questions.

**Sounds right, wrong logic.** "Moreover" and "however" both sound formal and natural. Only the relationship tells you which is correct.

**Reading too little.** Sometimes the blank connects to the main idea of the whole passage, not just the sentence right before it. If the two-sentence test gives you two possible relationships, read the passage from the beginning.

**Concession versus contrast.** "Admittedly" introduces a point that works *against* the author's argument, which the author then answers. "However" simply signals a turn. Check which one the passage's structure supports.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are transition words on the SAT?

They're words and phrases like "however," "therefore," "for example," and "in addition" that show how two ideas relate. SAT transitions questions ask you to choose the one that fits the logic of the passage.

### How do I answer SAT transitions questions quickly?

Read the sentences on both sides of the blank, name their relationship in one word before looking at the choices, then pick the transition from that group.

### What if two transition choices mean the same thing?

Then neither can be the single correct answer. Eliminate both and choose between the remaining options.

### Are transitions questions grammar questions?

No. They test logic, not grammar rules. They're part of the Expression of Ideas domain, while grammar and punctuation belong to Standard English Conventions.

### Which transition words should I know for the SAT?

Know at least one word from each group: contrast (however), addition (moreover), cause and effect (therefore), example (for instance), sequence (subsequently), and clarification (in other words). Knowing the groups matters more than memorizing long lists.
