<h2>How to Approach SAT Reading Questions: A Test-Day Decision Procedure</h2><p>When tackling <strong>sat reading questions</strong> that test central ideas and details within the Information and Ideas domain, you need a precise system. The Digital SAT Reading and Writing section contains <code>54</code> questions across two <code>32</code>-minute modules (<code>27</code> questions per module, which includes <code>2</code> unscored pretest questions per module). Because every question features a short, independent passage, running a standardized decision procedure prevents lost time and eliminates common distractor traps.</p><table><thead><tr><th>Test Section</th><th>Total Questions</th><th>Scored vs Unscored</th><th>Time Allowed</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Reading and Writing Module 1</td><td><code>27</code></td><td><code>25</code> scored / <code>2</code> unscored</td><td><code>32</code> minutes</td></tr><tr><td>Reading and Writing Module 2</td><td><code>27</code></td><td><code>25</code> scored / <code>2</code> unscored</td><td><code>32</code> minutes</td></tr><tr><td>Reading and Writing Total</td><td><code>54</code></td><td><code>50</code> scored / <code>4</code> unscored</td><td><code>64</code> minutes</td></tr></tbody></table><h3>Step 1: Is the question asking for the main idea, a specific detail, or a sentence function?</h3><p>First, check the stem to categorize the prompt. Stems asking for the main idea require a summary of the entire passage. Stems starting with <code>Based on the text...</code> or <code>According to the text...</code> target explicit details. Prompts asking <code>Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence?</code> ask what structural job that sentence performs in relation to the main argument. For broader strategies across all item types, consult our guide on <a href="/guides/sat-reading-practice-questions/">SAT Reading Practice Questions: How to Answer Each Question Type</a>.</p><h3>Step 2: Does the choice capture the full scope of the text, or is it too narrow?</h3><p>If the stem asks for the central idea, eliminate choices that only describe a single supporting sentence. A detail can be <code>100%</code> factually true according to the text, but if it omits the primary outcome or central thesis, it is incorrect.</p><p>Let's apply this step to a concrete example from our <a href="/sat/reading-writing/central-ideas-and-details/">central ideas and details practice bank</a>:</p><p><strong>Example 1 (Main Idea):</strong><br><em>For centuries, sailors relied on the stars to find their latitude, but longitude was much harder to determine at sea. Finding it required knowing the exact time at a reference location while at sea, and the pendulum clocks of the period did not keep accurate time on a rolling ship. In the eighteenth century, the English clockmaker John Harrison built a series of marine chronometers that kept time accurately enough to solve the problem.</em><br>Which choice best states the main idea of the text?</p><ul><li>Choice A: Harrison's marine chronometers solved the problem of finding longitude at sea by keeping accurate time on board.</li><li>Choice B: Sailors could not find their latitude until the eighteenth century.</li><li>Choice C: Pendulum clocks were the most accurate timekeepers available to sailors.</li><li>Choice D: John Harrison was the first person to use the stars for navigation.</li></ul><p><strong>Decision procedure breakdown:</strong> The passage introduces a historical problem (finding longitude at sea due to inaccurate pendulum clocks) and concludes with the resolution (John Harrison's marine chronometers solved it). Choice A correctly synthesizes both the problem and the resolution. Choice B directly contradicts the first sentence, which notes sailors could already find latitude using stars. Choice C focuses on pendulum clocks without addressing their failure on rolling ships or Harrison's solution. Choice D misstates Harrison's achievement. Thus, Choice A is the correct answer.</p><h3>Step 3: If the stem asks for a specific detail, is the fact directly stated in the text?</h3><p>Detail questions require no reading between the lines. Locate the specific mechanism or cause mentioned in the text and match it to the answer choice that rephrases that fact accurately.</p><p><strong>Example 2 (Explicit Detail):</strong><br><em>Coral reefs cover a tiny fraction of the ocean floor, yet they support roughly a quarter of all known marine species. Reef-building corals depend on algae that live inside their tissues and supply them with food. When water becomes too warm, corals expel these algae and turn white, a process called bleaching. If high temperatures last too long, the corals can die.</em><br>Based on the text, why are high water temperatures a threat to corals?</p><ul><li>Choice A: Warm water makes corals change color permanently without harming them.</li><li>Choice B: Warm water increases the number of species living on reefs.</li><li>Choice C: Warm water makes coral reefs cover more of the ocean floor.</li><li>Choice D: Warm water causes corals to expel the algae that supply their food.</li></ul><p><strong>Decision procedure breakdown:</strong> The prompt asks specifically why elevated temperatures threaten corals. Sentence <code>3</code> states that warm water causes corals to <code>expel these algae</code>, and sentence <code>2</code> explains that these algae <code>supply them with food</code>. Choice D directly restates this factual relationship. Choice A contradicts the text (prolonged heat can kill them). Choice B and Choice C introduce unsupported assertions not mentioned in the text. Choice D is correct.</p><h3>Step 4: If the stem asks for sentence function, how does that sentence connect to the sentence before and after it?</h3><p>When analyzing sentence function, determine whether the target sentence presents a contrast, provides evidence, defines a concept, or introduces a historical baseline. Check our <a href="/guides/sat-mock-test-review/">SAT Mock Test Review guide</a> for tracking structural errors during practice runs.</p><p><strong>Example 3 (Sentence Function):</strong><br><em>The printing press developed by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 used movable metal type, which could be rearranged to print any page. Before it, books in Europe were mostly copied by hand, a slow and costly process. Printed books could be produced far faster and more cheaply, and within decades presses had spread to cities across Europe.</em><br>Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence in the text?</p><ul><li>Choice A: It gives the date when the printing press was developed.</li><li>Choice B: It names the cities where presses spread.</li><li>Choice C: It describes how books were made before the printing press, to show what the press changed.</li><li>Choice D: It explains how movable metal type worked.</li></ul><p><strong>Decision procedure breakdown:</strong> The second sentence reads: <em>Before it, books in Europe were mostly copied by hand, a slow and costly process.</em> This sets up a baseline comparison for the third sentence, which details how printed books were far faster and cheaper. Choice C accurately describes this comparative role. Choice A applies to sentence <code>1</code> (around <code>1440</code>). Choice B applies to sentence <code>3</code>. Choice D references movable type from sentence <code>1</code> without describing the second sentence's job. Choice C is correct.</p><h2>How to Solve Hard SAT Reading Questions on Test Day</h2><p>To master hard <strong>sat reading questions</strong> under time constraints, keep these three operational rules in mind during your test:</p><ol><li><strong>Identify the scope:</strong> If two choices look accurate, measure their scope. Main idea choices must cover the central resolution of the text, not just a supporting premise.</li><li><strong>Beware of false details:</strong> Distractors often use words lifted directly from the passage but alter the relationship between them (e.g., swapping cause and effect).</li><li><strong>Track your time budget:</strong> You have <code>32</code> minutes for <code>27</code> questions in each Reading and Writing module, giving you roughly <code>71</code> seconds per question. If a passage seems ambiguous, eliminate clear wrong answers, make a selection, flag it, and return to it if time remains.</li></ol><p>For further test-wide structure details, including Math domain timing (<code>70</code> minutes for <code>44</code> questions across two <code>35</code>-minute modules), review our overview of the <a href="/guides/digital-sat-format/">Digital SAT Format</a>. <h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>How to answer SAT reading questions on central ideas?</h3>
<p>Identify the primary topic and the author's main point, then eliminate choices that focus on minor details or contradict the passage. The Digital SAT Reading and Writing section gives you 32 minutes for 27 questions per module (64 minutes total across 54 questions), so quickly evaluating whether a choice covers the entire text is key.</p>
<h3>How to get better at SAT reading questions on test day?</h3>
<p>Practice identifying structural transitions and sentence functions across short passages. On the Digital SAT, scores for Reading and Writing range from 200 to 800, and standard modules contain 25 scored questions plus 2 unscored pretest questions.</p>
<h3>How many SAT reading questions are on the test?</h3>
<p>The Reading and Writing section contains 54 total questions split evenly across two 27-question modules. Across the entire exam, which includes Math's 44 questions, you will encounter 98 total questions in 134 minutes of testing time.</p>
