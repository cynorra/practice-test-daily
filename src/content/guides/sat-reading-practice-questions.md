The SAT reading questions on the Digital SAT look different from the long passages of the old paper test. Each question in the Reading and Writing section has its own short text, usually a single paragraph, sometimes two related texts or a table, followed by one question with four choices. That changes how you should practice. Instead of building stamina for long passages, SAT reading practice is about recognizing the question type quickly and knowing what kind of evidence it needs.

Below are the main reading question types, each with an original example written in SAT style and a full explanation.

## A method that works on every reading question

1. **Read the question before the passage.** It tells you what you're looking for: a word's meaning, the main idea, a logical completion.
2. **Read the passage once, carefully.** It's short, so you don't need to skim.
3. **Predict the answer in your own words.** Do this before you look at the choices.
4. **Eliminate choices that go too far.** Wrong answers on the SAT are often *almost* right: true but off-topic, too extreme, or about the wrong part of the text.
5. **Point to the line.** Before you pick, be able to point to the words in the passage that support your choice.

## Words in context

These questions ask for the most logical and precise word or phrase to fill a blank, or what a word means as it's used in the text.

> The architect's early sketches were deliberately ______: she drew only rough outlines, leaving the details to be worked out once the client had chosen a direction.
>
> A) meticulous<br>B) provisional<br>C) extravagant<br>D) hostile

**Answer: B.** The clue is after the colon: rough outlines, details left for later. "Provisional," meaning temporary and open to change, fits. "Meticulous" means the opposite. "Extravagant" and "hostile" have nothing to do with how finished the sketches were. Notice that the clue was in the sentence itself; it almost always is. More of these are in the [words in context practice set](/sat/reading-writing/words-in-context/).

## Text structure and purpose

These questions ask what a sentence does in the passage or how the whole text is organized.

> Many people assume that desert plants survive mainly by storing water. Some do. But a large group of desert shrubs takes the opposite approach: they drop their leaves during droughts and stay nearly dormant until rain returns.
>
> Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence ("Some do.")?

**Answer:** It concedes that the common assumption is partly true before the text introduces a different strategy. A choice like "It rejects the claim made in the first sentence" would be wrong, because the sentence agrees with part of it. Purpose questions are about the *job* a sentence does, so look at what comes before and after it.

## Cross-text connections

You get two short texts on the same topic and a question about how one author would respond to the other. The key is to pin down each author's actual claim in a few words before reading the choices. If Text 1 says a policy worked and Text 2 says the data behind that conclusion was incomplete, the answer will say Text 2's author would question the evidence, not necessarily the conclusion.

## Central ideas and details

These ask for the main idea of a text or for a detail the text states.

> A city replaced the asphalt in several schoolyards with grass, trees, and shaded areas. Over the following summers, surface temperatures in those yards were measured as consistently lower than in nearby paved lots, and teachers reported that students spent more time outside during hot afternoons.
>
> Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

**Answer:** Redesigning schoolyards with plants and shade made them cooler and more usable in hot weather. A choice that mentions only the temperature data is too narrow, and a choice claiming that all cities should remove asphalt goes beyond the text. The main idea has to cover the whole passage and stay inside what it actually says. Practice: [central ideas and details](/sat/reading-writing/central-ideas-and-details/).

## Command of evidence: text

These questions state a claim and ask which quotation or finding would best support it, or weaken it. Treat it like matching: restate the claim in your own words, then find the choice that proves *that exact claim*. A quotation can be true, interesting, and from the right text and still fail, because it supports a different point.

## Command of evidence: data

Here the passage comes with a table or graph, and the question asks which choice uses the data to complete a statement. Two habits prevent most misses:

- **Read the labels and units first.** Know what each row, column, and axis means before you read any numbers.
- **Check the comparison the question asks for.** If the claim is about change over time, a choice that compares two groups in a single year doesn't answer it, even if the numbers in it are correct.

## Inferences

Inference questions end with a blank and ask for the choice that most logically completes the text. The right answer follows from the passage without needing outside information.

> Researchers expected birds in the noisier part of the park to sing at a higher pitch, since higher notes are easier to hear over low traffic noise. Recordings showed that birds in both parts of the park sang at about the same pitch. This finding suggests that ______
>
> A) traffic noise has no effect on any bird species.<br>
> B) the birds in the noisier area did not raise their pitch in response to the noise.<br>
> C) birds in quiet areas sing more often than birds in noisy areas.<br>
> D) higher notes are harder to hear than lower notes.

**Answer: B.** The researchers predicted a difference, and there wasn't one, so the birds didn't change their pitch. Choice A is far too broad ("any bird species"), choice C brings in how often birds sing, which the text never measured, and choice D contradicts the passage.

## How to practice SAT reading

Because each question is self-contained, you can practice one type at a time. That's much more efficient than reading long passages and answering a mix of everything. A good order:

1. Start with [words in context](/sat/reading-writing/words-in-context/) and [central ideas](/sat/reading-writing/central-ideas-and-details/): they build the habit of finding the clue in the text.
2. Add inference and evidence questions once you can reliably point to the supporting line.
3. Mix types in timed sets closer to test day. The Reading and Writing section gives you 32 minutes for each module of 27 questions, so most reading questions should take about a minute.

The writing side of the section, meaning grammar, transitions, and synthesis, is covered in the [SAT grammar rules guide](/guides/sat-grammar-rules/), and the full section layout is in the [Digital SAT format guide](/guides/digital-sat-format/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a separate reading test on the SAT?

No. On the Digital SAT, reading and writing are combined into one Reading and Writing section with 54 questions in two modules. Reading questions and writing questions are mixed within each module.

### How long are SAT reading passages?

Each question has its own short passage, usually a single paragraph. Some questions pair two short texts or add a table or graph.

### How can I get faster at SAT reading questions?

Read the question first, predict the answer before looking at the choices, and practice one question type at a time until you recognize it immediately. Speed comes from recognizing the type, not from skimming.

### Do I need outside knowledge for SAT reading?

No. Every correct answer is supported by the passage itself. If a choice needs information the text doesn't give, it's wrong.

### What is the most common mistake on SAT reading questions?

Choosing an answer that's true or reasonable but doesn't match what the question asks, such as a detail when the question wants the main idea, or a claim that goes further than the text.
