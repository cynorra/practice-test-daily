<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> The CEFR (Common European Framework of Reference for Languages) divides language ability into six levels: A1 and A2 (basic user), B1 and B2 (independent user), and C1 and C2 (proficient user). Each level describes what a learner can do with the language, not how many words they know. You can estimate your level with a practice test, but only an official exam gives a certified result.</p></div>

When a university, an employer or a language school asks for "B2 English", they are using the CEFR. It is the most widely used scale for describing language ability in Europe and is used by many schools and exams elsewhere. This guide explains what each level means in practice, what you can realistically do at each one, and how exam scores relate to the scale.

A note on sources: the level descriptions below are our own summary in plain English. They are not quotations from the official framework. For the official text, see the Council of Europe's CEFR pages, linked at the end.

## The three groups and six levels

The six levels fall into three groups. ETS uses the same grouping in its TOEFL iBT test specifications: Basic (A1–A2), Independent (B1–B2) and Proficient (C1–C2).

## What each level feels like

**A1.** You can introduce yourself, ask and answer simple questions about where you live and what you do, and understand slow, clear speech. Reading is limited to signs, short messages and forms. A typical A1 question asks you to choose between "is" and "are".

**A2.** You can describe your family, your job and your daily routine in simple sentences, buy things in a shop and write a short thank-you note. You understand common words in short texts but need time. Past tenses and comparisons start to appear.

**B1.** You can talk about experiences, plans and opinions, follow the main points of clear speech on familiar topics and write connected paragraphs. You make mistakes, but people understand you. At B1, conditionals and the present perfect become important.

**B2.** You can follow longer texts and lectures, take part in discussions without long pauses and write clear, detailed texts. Programs that teach in English set their own requirements, expressed as a CEFR level or as an exam score, so check the one you are applying to.

**C1.** You can understand long, demanding texts, pick up implied meaning, speak fluently without searching for words and write well-structured texts. Mistakes are occasional.

**C2.** You can summarize information from different sources, express yourself precisely and understand nearly everything you hear or read.

## Skills are not always at the same level

Learners often have an uneven profile. Someone who reads at B2 may speak at B1, and someone who lived abroad may speak at B2 but write at A2. When a school or an exam gives you one level, it is usually an overall summary.

## How exams relate to the CEFR

Exam providers report scores on their own scales and then link them to CEFR levels. TOEFL is a good example. ETS says that TOEFL iBT score reports for tests taken from January 21, 2026 use a 1–6 scale in half-point steps, "with a more intuitive alignment" to the CEFR. We cover the format and the scale on our [TOEFL practice page](/toefl/).

Because each exam publishes its own tables, do not guess a conversion. If you need to know what a score means in CEFR terms, use the table published by the exam provider.

## How to estimate your own level

1. **Take a level test.** Our [English level test](/english/english-level-test/) has five questions for each level from A1 to C2 and shows an estimated band at the end.
2. **Read the explanations** for each question you miss and note which level the question belonged to.
3. **Check your other skills.** A written test cannot measure speaking and listening, so try speaking with a partner or a teacher.
4. **Retest after a month** of study to see whether the estimate has moved.

A practice test is an estimate. Only an official exam taken at an approved center gives a certified level.

## Frequently asked questions

### What CEFR level do I need to study at a university in English?

Requirements vary by university and program, and each one decides which level or exam score it asks for. Check the official requirement of the program and the exams it accepts.

### Is there a CEFR level between B1 and B2?

The official scale has six levels. Some schools and tests use labels such as B1+, but those are the school's own sublevels and are not part of the six official levels.

### Can I move from A2 to B2 in one year?

It depends on how much time you study and how you use it, and there is no guaranteed schedule. Judge progress by what you can do, not by a deadline.

### Is C2 the same as being a native speaker?

No. C2 describes a very high level of mastery in all skills, but it is a measure of what a learner can do, not a comparison with native speakers.
