<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> English has twelve tenses: three time frames (past, present, future) combined with four aspects (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous). The time frame says when something happens; the aspect says how you see it: as a fact (simple), as in progress (continuous), as completed before another point (perfect), or as in progress up to that point (perfect continuous).</p></div>

Learners often study tenses as a long list of forms and forget what they are for. A chart is more useful when you read it as a grid: pick the time frame, pick the aspect, and the tense follows. This guide gives you the full chart, one use for each tense, and the verb mistakes we see most often. When you want to test yourself, use the [verb tense test](/english/grammar-test/verbs/).

## Choosing between similar tenses

**Past simple or present perfect?** Use the past simple when the time is finished and stated (yesterday, in 2019, last week). Use the present perfect when the time is not stated or when the result matters now (I have seen that film). "I have seen it yesterday" is wrong because "yesterday" is a finished time.

**Present perfect with "for" and "since".** Use "since" with a starting point (since 2019, since Monday) and "for" with a length of time (for five years, for two hours). "She has lived here since 2019" and "She has lived here for six years" are both correct.

**Past simple or past continuous?** The past simple reports a completed event. The past continuous describes what was in progress when something else happened: "I was walking home when it started to rain."

**Will or going to?** Use "will" for a decision made at the moment of speaking and for general predictions. Use "going to" for a plan you already have or for a prediction based on evidence you can see now.

## Time clauses

After "when", "as soon as", "before", "after" and "until", do not use "will" to talk about the future. Use the present simple: "I'll call you as soon as I get home", not "as soon as I will get home".

## Irregular verbs you will meet again and again

Regular verbs add -ed in the past and the past participle. Irregular verbs have their own forms, and they are the source of many errors.

<thead><tr><th>Base</th><th>Past simple</th><th>Past participle</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>be</td><td>was / were</td><td>been</td></tr>
<tr><td>go</td><td>went</td><td>gone</td></tr>
<tr><td>see</td><td>saw</td><td>seen</td></tr>
<tr><td>swim</td><td>swam</td><td>swum</td></tr>
<tr><td>rise</td><td>rose</td><td>risen</td></tr>
<tr><td>lay (put down)</td><td>laid</td><td>laid</td></tr>

<tr><td>write</td><td>wrote</td><td>written</td></tr>

"Lay" and "lie" are easy to confuse: "lay" needs an object (She laid her coat on the chair), "lie" does not (He lay on the sofa).

## Subject-verb agreement

The verb agrees with the subject, not with the noun closest to it. In "The list of items is on the desk", the subject is "list", which is singular. With "neither ... nor", the verb agrees with the nearer subject: "Neither the manager nor the employees were aware." "News" is uncountable and takes a singular verb: "The news was good."

## Frequently asked questions

### Does English really have only twelve tenses?

The twelve-tense chart is a common teaching model that combines three time frames with four aspects. Some grammars count tenses differently, because English marks only past and present on the verb itself, and builds the others with auxiliary verbs.

### Which tense should I learn first?

Start with the present simple, the past simple and the present continuous, since they cover most everyday sentences. Then add the present perfect, which is the one that learners find hardest.

### How do I know whether an action is "continuous" or "simple"?

Ask whether you want to present the action as a whole or as something in progress. "I read the report" presents it as a whole; "I was reading the report when you called" presents it as in progress.

### What is the passive voice and is it a tense?

The passive is a voice, not a tense. It can be used with most tenses, using a form of "be" and a past participle: "The window was broken."
