<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> English pronouns change form with their job in the sentence: subject (I, he), object (me, him), possessive determiner (my, his), possessive pronoun (mine, his) and reflexive (myself, himself). Use the subject form before a verb, the object form after a verb or preposition, and a reflexive only when the doer and the receiver are the same person.</p></div>

Pronouns are small words, but they cause a large share of the mistakes that learners and native speakers make in writing. The reason is simple: the same person can appear as "I", "me", "my", "mine" or "myself", and only one of those fits a given sentence. This guide gives you the forms, the rules that choose between them, and the common traps.

## Rule 1: Subject or object?

Use the subject form when the pronoun does the action and comes before the verb. Use the object form when it receives the action or follows a preposition.

- She called **me**. (object of "called")
- **I** called her. (subject)
- This gift is for **him**. (after the preposition "for")

When two people are joined with "and", remove the other person and listen: "My brother and me are going" becomes "me are going", which sounds wrong, so the correct sentence is "My brother and **I** are going". The same test works for objects: "She invited my brother and me" is right because "She invited me" is right.

## Rule 2: After a preposition, use the object form

"Between you and I" is a common mistake. "Between" is a preposition, so it takes the object form: **between you and me**. The same goes for "to", "with", "for" and "from": give it to Sara and **me**.

## Rule 3: Possessive determiner or possessive pronoun?

A possessive determiner (my, your, his, her, its, our, their) must be followed by a noun. A possessive pronoun (mine, yours, hers, ours, theirs) stands alone.

- This is **my** book. / This book is **mine**.
- Is that **their** car? / Is that car **theirs**?

Note that none of the possessive forms takes an apostrophe. "Its" is the possessive of "it"; "it's" is a short form of "it is" or "it has". "Their's" does not exist.

## Rule 4: Reflexive pronouns

Use a reflexive pronoun in two cases.

1. **The subject and the object are the same person:** "He taught himself to play the guitar." "They introduced themselves."
2. **For emphasis:** "I prefer to pay for everything myself."

Do not use a reflexive just because it sounds formal. "Please contact myself" is wrong; write "Please contact me". And do not use it as a subject: "My brother and myself are going" is wrong.

## Rule 5: Who, whom and whose

- **Who** is the subject: The woman **who** lives next door is a pilot.
- **Whom** is the object, often after a preposition: To **whom** did you give the keys?
- **Whose** shows possession: He is the man **whose** car was stolen.

In everyday speech people use "who" for both subject and object, and in a relative clause "who" or "that" is often used where "whom" would be correct in formal writing. In an exam or a formal text, use "whom" after a preposition.

## Rule 6: Singular "they"

"They" and "their" are widely accepted for one person whose gender is unknown or not relevant, as in "Everyone should bring their own laptop". Some formal style guides prefer rewriting the sentence, for example "All participants should bring their own laptops". Follow the style guide of your school or publication.

## Rule 7: Make the reference clear

A pronoun should point clearly to one person or thing. "When Jo met Kim, she gave her the keys" leaves the reader guessing who "she" and "her" are. Fix it by repeating the names or rewriting: "When Jo met Kim, Jo gave Kim the keys."

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it "me and him" or "he and I"?

Use the form that fits the job of the pronouns. As the subject, it is "he and I went to the store". As the object, it is "the teacher called him and me".

### Can I start a sentence with "myself"?

No. "Myself" is a reflexive pronoun, so it needs a subject that refers to the same person, or it is used for emphasis after a noun or pronoun.

### What is the difference between "its" and "it's"?

"Its" shows possession (the dog wagged its tail). "It's" is short for "it is" or "it has" (it's raining).

### Why does "whom" still appear in writing?

It marks the object of a verb or a preposition in formal English. In speech it is rare, but formal writing and exams still expect it.
