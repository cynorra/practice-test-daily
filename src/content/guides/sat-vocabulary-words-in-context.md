SAT vocabulary isn't tested with standalone definitions anymore. There are no analogy questions or sentence completions with obscure words. Instead, words in context questions give you a short passage with a blank, or a word used in a specific sense, and ask for the most logical and precise choice. That means two things matter: knowing common academic words well, and reading the passage closely enough to see which meaning fits.

This guide explains how these questions work, gives you a list of 30 SAT vocabulary words that show up often in academic writing, each with an example sentence, and ends with a short SAT vocab quiz.

## How words in context questions work

A typical question looks like this:

> Although the committee praised the proposal's goals, its response to the budget was notably ______, approving only a small trial run instead of the full program.
>
> A) enthusiastic<br>B) restrained<br>C) indifferent<br>D) hostile

**Answer: B.** The clue is "approving only a small trial run instead of the full program": the committee was cautious, but it didn't reject the idea. "Restrained" fits exactly. "Enthusiastic" contradicts the clue. "Indifferent" means not caring, but the committee did care enough to approve a trial. "Hostile" is too strong.

Notice how the wrong answers work. One is the opposite, one is close but off in meaning, and one is too extreme. That pattern repeats across the whole question type, and it's why knowing a word's exact shade of meaning matters more than knowing a long list of rare words.

## A method for every vocabulary question

1. **Cover the choices.** Read the passage and find the clue: a contrast word ("although," "but," "instead"), a definition after a colon, or an example that shows what the blank must mean.
2. **Put your own word in the blank.** Something simple, like "cautious" or "temporary."
3. **Match the choices to your word.** Eliminate opposites first, then words that are too strong or too weak.
4. **Reread the sentence with your answer.** It should sound precise, not just possible.

## Words the SAT likes to pair as traps

Many vocabulary questions put two near-synonyms side by side. Knowing the difference is often the whole question:

- **tentative vs. hesitant:** a *plan* is tentative (not final); a *person* is hesitant (reluctant to act).
- **refute vs. dispute:** to *refute* is to prove wrong; to *dispute* is only to argue against.
- **corroborate vs. substantiate:** *corroborate* means confirm with additional evidence; *substantiate* means support a claim with evidence in the first place.
- **cursory vs. concise:** *cursory* is careless and quick; *concise* is brief but complete.
- **novel vs. obscure:** *novel* means new; *obscure* means little known, which may be old.

## A short SAT vocab quiz

Choose the most precise word for each blank. Answers follow.

1. The historian's conclusion was ______: she noted that the letters suggested a date but that more sources were needed to be sure.<br>A) definitive B) tentative C) arbitrary D) candid
2. Engineers added flexible joints to ______ the damage an earthquake could cause.<br>A) mitigate B) corroborate C) discern D) concede
3. Although the critic disliked the film's ending, she ______ that its first hour was gripping.<br>A) refuted B) hindered C) conceded D) scrutinized
4. The first study had only 12 participants, so researchers were cautious about trying to ______ its results to the whole population.<br>A) extrapolate B) undermine C) mitigate D) substantiate
5. The detective's ______ inspection of the room missed the open window entirely.<br>A) meticulous B) impartial C) cursory D) pragmatic

**Answers:** 1-B (the conclusion isn't final; "definitive" is the opposite), 2-A (make the damage less severe), 3-C (she admits a point against her overall view; "refuted" would mean she proved it wrong), 4-A (extend findings beyond the data), 5-C (the inspection missed something obvious, so it was quick and careless; "meticulous" is the opposite).

## How to build SAT vocabulary that sticks

- **Read academic writing.** Science news, long-form history, and essays use these words naturally, and you'll meet them in context the way the SAT presents them.
- **Learn words in sentences.** Write your own example for each new word. A definition alone fades fast.
- **Practice the question type.** The [words in context practice set](/sat/reading-writing/words-in-context/) gives you SAT-style questions with an explanation for every choice, which is where you learn the shades of meaning.
- **Keep a short list of misses.** When you miss a word in practice, add it with the sentence it came from and review the list weekly.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is vocabulary still on the SAT?

Yes, but only in context. The Digital SAT tests vocabulary through words in context questions in the Reading and Writing section, where you choose the most precise word for a passage.

### Should I memorize an SAT vocabulary list?

A short list of common academic words, learned in sentences, is useful. Memorizing hundreds of rare words is not a good use of time, because the test focuses on precise meaning in context rather than obscure definitions.

### How many vocabulary questions are on the SAT?

There's no fixed number for each question type, and the mix varies from test to test. Words in context questions are part of the Craft and Structure domain, alongside text structure and cross-text questions.

### What is the best way to practice SAT words?

Practice real words in context questions, read the explanation for every wrong choice, and keep a list of words you miss with the sentence they appeared in.

### Are SAT vocabulary words hard?

Most are common academic words rather than rare ones. The difficulty comes from choosing between near-synonyms, so focus on exact meanings.
