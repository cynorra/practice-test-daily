<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Adult learners most often go wrong on articles (a, an, the), prepositions, countable and uncountable nouns (much and many, fewer and less), since and for, verb patterns (enjoy doing, suggest doing) and word order in reported questions. Each of these has a short rule, and most mistakes disappear once you learn the rule and check your own writing against it.</p></div>

Adult learners usually understand grammar well enough to be understood, but some mistakes persist for years, because they do not interfere with meaning and nobody corrects them. This guide lists the errors that appear again and again, the rule behind each one and a fix. Use it as a checklist for your own writing. To test yourself afterward, take our [English grammar test](/english/grammar-test/).

## 1. Countable and uncountable nouns

Some nouns can be counted (books, chairs, ideas) and some cannot (advice, information, furniture, news). Uncountable nouns have no plural and no "a" or "an".

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead><tr><th>Incorrect</th><th>Correct</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>She gave me an advice.</td><td>She gave me some advice. / a piece of advice.</td></tr>
<tr><td>I need some informations.</td><td>I need some information.</td></tr>
<tr><td>There aren't many sugar left.</td><td>There isn't much sugar left.</td></tr>
<tr><td>There are less students than last year.</td><td>There are fewer students than last year.</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>

Use "much" and "less" with uncountable nouns, and "many" and "fewer" with countable plural nouns.

## 2. Prepositions

Prepositions rarely translate word for word from another language, so learn them in set phrases.

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead><tr><th>Incorrect</th><th>Correct</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>I'm interested on art.</td><td>I'm interested in art.</td></tr>
<tr><td>She is good in math.</td><td>She is good at math.</td></tr>
<tr><td>We arrived to the airport.</td><td>We arrived at the airport.</td></tr>
<tr><td>The meeting is in Monday.</td><td>The meeting is on Monday.</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>

A simple guide for time: "at" for clock times (at 9 a.m.), "on" for days and dates (on Monday), "in" for months, years and longer periods (in March, in 2025).

## 3. Since and for

Use "since" with a starting point and "for" with a length of time. Both usually go with the present perfect.

- She has lived here **since** 2019.
- She has lived here **for** six years.

## 4. Verb patterns: -ing or to?

Some verbs are followed by the -ing form and some by an infinitive. You have to learn them in groups.

- **-ing:** enjoy, suggest, mind, avoid, finish, look forward to (where "to" is a preposition)
- **to + verb:** decide, hope, want, plan, manage

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead><tr><th>Incorrect</th><th>Correct</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>He suggested to take a taxi.</td><td>He suggested taking a taxi.</td></tr>
<tr><td>We enjoyed to explore the town.</td><td>We enjoyed exploring the town.</td></tr>
<tr><td>I look forward to see you.</td><td>I look forward to seeing you.</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>

A few verbs change meaning with each pattern: "stop to ask" means stop in order to ask, while "stop asking" means quit asking.

## 5. Articles

Use "a" or "an" for one of many things that the listener cannot identify yet, and "the" for something the listener can identify. Do not use an article before plural or uncountable nouns that you mean in general.

- I bought **a** book. **The** book was about trains.
- **Water** is essential. (in general, no article)
- **The** water in this lake is cold. (a specific lake)

## 6. Word order in questions inside sentences

In a direct question, the verb comes before the subject ("Where is the library?"). Inside a longer sentence, the order goes back to subject and then verb.

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead><tr><th>Incorrect</th><th>Correct</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Could you tell me where is the library?</td><td>Could you tell me where the library is?</td></tr>
<tr><td>She asked me where had I been.</td><td>She asked me where I had been.</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>

## 7. Pronouns

The common error is choosing the subject form where the object form is needed, or the reverse: "between you and I" should be "between you and me", and "me and my friend went" should be "my friend and I went". Our guide to [English pronouns](/guides/english-pronouns-rules/) gives the full rules and a table.

## 8. Tenses

Do not use the present perfect with a finished time: "I have seen him yesterday" should be "I saw him yesterday". After "if" in a real condition, use the present, not "will": "If I see him, I'll tell him". The [verb tenses chart](/guides/english-verb-tenses-chart/) shows all twelve tenses.

## 9. Adjective and adverb forms

Use an adjective to describe a noun and an adverb to describe a verb: "He drives carefully" (not "careful"). Use the -ing adjective for the thing that causes the feeling and the -ed adjective for the person who feels it: "The film was boring. I was bored."

## 10. Comparatives and superlatives

Short adjectives take -er and -est (taller, tallest). Long adjectives take "more" and "most" (more interesting, most generous). Do not combine both: "more taller" is wrong.

## How to use this list

1. Pick two rules that you recognize from your own writing.
2. Look for those mistakes in an email or paragraph you wrote last week.
3. Write five new sentences that use each rule correctly.
4. Take the [grammar test](/english/grammar-test/) and see whether the same rules appear in your mistakes.
5. Repeat the next week with two other rules.

Working on two rules at a time is slow but it works, because you can finally notice and correct the mistake in your own writing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which grammar mistake is the most common among adult learners?

It varies by first language, but errors with articles and prepositions are very common, since many languages handle those words differently from English.

### Do I need to learn every grammar rule to speak well?

No. Focus on the rules that affect your writing and speaking most, and learn the others as you meet them. Most learners improve fastest by fixing a few recurring mistakes.

### How can I find my own recurring mistakes?

Collect the corrections from your teacher or from a language exchange partner, and group them by rule. Patterns usually appear within a few weeks.

### Is it a mistake to end a sentence with a preposition?

No. "Who did you give it to?" is natural English, and in formal writing "To whom did you give it?" is also correct. Choose the form that suits your audience.
