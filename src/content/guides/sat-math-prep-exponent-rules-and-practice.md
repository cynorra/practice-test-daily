<h2>Belief 1: You Should Multiply Coefficients the Same Way You Multiply Exponents</h2><p>A frequent error during <strong>sat math prep</strong> is applying variable exponent laws directly to numerical coefficients. When an expression like <code>(5x⁶)³</code> is raised to a power, students often multiply the coefficient by the outer exponent to get <code>15</code> instead of raising the coefficient to that power. In reality, every factor inside the parentheses is raised to the outer power independently: <code>5³ = 125</code>, while the variable power uses the power-of-a-power rule <code>(x⁶)³ = x⁶ ˣ ³ = x¹⁸</code>.</p><p>Understanding this distinction is vital for the digital SAT Math section, which consists of <code>44</code> total questions split evenly across two <code>22</code>-question modules (with <code>35</code> minutes allocated per module). The Math section covers four primary content domains: Algebra, Advanced Math, Problem-Solving and Data Analysis, and Geometry and Trigonometry. Equivalent expressions involving exponents fall directly into the Advanced Math domain, where precision with algebraic terms is essential to earning points toward your <code>200</code> to <code>800</code> Math section score.</p><p>To build confidence on these questions, examine this worked practice item from our <a href="/sat/math/exponents-and-equivalent-expressions/">exponent rules and equivalent expressions practice</a> set:</p><p><strong>Question 1:</strong> Which expression is equivalent to <code>(5x⁶)³</code>?</p><ul><li>A) <code>5x¹⁸</code></li><li>B) <code>125x⁹</code></li><li>C) <code>125x¹⁸</code></li><li>D) <code>15x¹⁸</code></li></ul><p><strong>Correct Answer:</strong> <code>125x¹⁸</code></p><p><strong>Detailed Solution:</strong> Apply the power of a product rule, <code>(ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ</code>. First, raise the constant coefficient to the power of <code>3</code>: <code>5³ = 5 · 5 · 5 = 125</code>. Next, apply the power of a power rule to the variable component: <code>(x⁶)³ = x⁶ ˣ ³ = x¹⁸</code>. Combining these components yields <code>125x¹⁸</code>. Option D (<code>15x¹⁸</code>) represents the common trap where the coefficient <code>5</code> is multiplied by <code>3</code> rather than raised to the power of <code>3</code>. Option B (<code>125x⁹</code>) represents the mistake of adding exponents <code>6 + 3 = 9</code> instead of multiplying them.</p><p>Another variation of this exact pattern reinforces why coefficient evaluation must be isolated from variable rules:</p><p><strong>Question 2:</strong> Which expression is equivalent to <code>(4x⁶)³</code>?</p><ul><li>A) <code>12x¹⁸</code></li><li>B) <code>4x¹⁸</code></li><li>C) <code>64x¹⁸</code></li><li>D) <code>64x⁹</code></li></ul><p><strong>Correct Answer:</strong> <code>64x¹⁸</code></p><p><strong>Detailed Solution:</strong> Separate the expression into its numeric coefficient and variable terms: <code>4³ · (x⁶)³</code>. Calculating <code>4³</code> gives <code>4 · 4 · 4 = 64</code>. Multiplying the variable exponents gives <code>6 · 3 = 18</code>, yielding <code>x¹⁸</code>. Putting both parts together results in <code>64x¹⁸</code>. Choice A (<code>12x¹⁸</code>) incorrectly multiplies <code>4 · 3</code>, while Choice B forgets to cube the coefficient altogether.</p><h2>Belief 2: Exponents Should Be Added Whenever You Work With Rational Expressions</h2><p>Many test takers assume that all exponent operations involve adding power values regardless of whether terms are multiplied, divided, or stacked in rational expressions. The product rule states that when multiplying terms with identical bases, you add exponents: <code>xᵃ · xᵇ = xᵃ ⁺ ᵇ</code>. However, the quotient rule dictates that when dividing terms with identical bases, you must subtract the denominator exponent from the numerator exponent: <code>xᵃ / xᵇ = xᵃ ⁻ ᵇ</code>.</p><p>Failing to apply the quotient rule correctly leads to choosing wrong answer choices on multi-step fraction problems. You can review full test strategies in our guide on <a href="/guides/sat-math-practice-questions/">SAT math practice questions</a> and explore overall test structure in our breakdown of the <a href="/guides/digital-sat-format/">digital SAT format</a>.</p><p>Let's walk through an exemplar problem that combines product and quotient exponent rules:</p><p><strong>Question 3:</strong> Which expression is equivalent to <code>(x³ · x⁹) / x²</code>, where <code>x &gt; 0</code>?</p><ul><li>A) <code>x¹⁰</code></li><li>B) <code>x¹⁴</code></li><li>C) <code>x¹³</code></li><li>D) <code>x²⁵</code></li></ul><p><strong>Correct Answer:</strong> <code>x¹⁰</code></p><p><strong>Detailed Solution:</strong> First, simplify the numerator by applying the product rule for exponents: <code>x³ · x⁹ = x³ ⁺ ⁹ = x¹²</code>. Next, simplify the resulting quotient <code>x¹² / x²</code> using the quotient rule: <code>x¹² ⁻ ² = x¹⁰</code>. The final simplified form is <code>x¹⁰</code>. Choice B (<code>x¹⁴</code>) comes from mistakenly adding the denominator exponent (<code>12 + 2 = 14</code>). Choice C (<code>x¹³</code>) comes from adding all three numbers together (<code>3 + 9 + 2 = 14</code>) and miscalculating by one.</p><p>Now try a question that mixes a coefficient with the quotient rule:</p>
<p><strong>Question 4:</strong> Which expression is equivalent to <code>(18x⁷) / (3x²)</code>, where <code>x &gt; 0</code>?</p>
<ul><li>A) <code>6x⁵</code></li><li>B) <code>15x⁵</code></li><li>C) <code>6x⁹</code></li><li>D) <code>6x³·⁵</code></li></ul><p><strong>Correct Answer:</strong> <code>6x⁵</code></p>
<p><strong>Detailed Solution:</strong> Treat the coefficients and the variable separately. Divide the coefficients: <code>18 ÷ 3 = 6</code>. Apply the quotient rule to the variable: <code>x⁷ / x² = x⁷ ⁻ ² = x⁵</code>. The result is <code>6x⁵</code>. Choice B (<code>15x⁵</code>) subtracts the coefficients (<code>18 − 3 = 15</code>) instead of dividing them. Choice C (<code>6x⁹</code>) adds the exponents (<code>7 + 2 = 9</code>) when the quotient rule subtracts them. Choice D (<code>6x³·⁵</code>) divides the exponents (<code>7 ÷ 2 = 3.5</code>), which no exponent rule allows.</p>
