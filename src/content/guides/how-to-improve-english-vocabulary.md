<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Learn a small number of words each day (five to ten), meet each one in a sentence, use it in a sentence of your own, and review it after one day, one week and one month. Take a vocabulary test at the start and end of the 30 days to see what changed. Depth matters more than volume: a word you can use is worth more than ten you only recognize.</p></div>

Most learners know the feeling of recognizing a word in a text and then failing to use it in a conversation. Closing that gap takes a plan, not more word lists. This guide gives you a 30-day routine that takes about 20 to 30 minutes a day, with a way to measure whether it works.

If you do not know your starting level, take our [English level test](/english/english-level-test/) first and read [what the CEFR levels mean](/guides/english-level-test-cefr-levels/). That tells you whether to start with everyday words or with academic vocabulary.

## Principles that make words stick

**Learn words in context.** A word on its own is hard to remember and easy to misuse. A sentence shows the grammar around the word and the situations where it fits.

**Learn the whole word.** For each new word, note the part of speech, one common phrase it appears in and any word family (for example: persuade, persuasion, persuasive).

**Use it.** Say or write at least one sentence about your own life with each new word. This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that moves a word from recognition to use.

**Review on a schedule.** Look at each word again after about a day, a week and a month. Short, spaced reviews are less tiring than long cramming sessions.

If you prefer flashcards, put the sentence on the front with the new word hidden, and the word, its meaning and your own sentence on the back. A paper stack or an app works equally well, as long as you review on a schedule.

Adjust the numbers to your life. If five words a day feels like too much, use three, and keep the routine. A small habit that you keep is better than a big plan that you drop in the second week.

## Where to find words worth learning

- **Texts at the right difficulty.** Choose reading where you understand most of the words. Skip words you meet once and never see again; collect the ones that appear two or three times.
- **Your own interests.** A learner who loves cooking will remember "simmer" and "marinate" faster than words from a random list.
- **Your goals.** If you are preparing for an exam or a degree, read texts from that field and collect the words that repeat in them.

## Common mistakes

1. **Learning definitions only.** Knowing a translation is not the same as knowing how to use the word.
2. **Collecting too many words.** A list of 200 words you never review is useless.
3. **Never testing yourself.** Reading a list feels productive, but only recall shows what you know.
4. **Ignoring pronunciation.** Say each word aloud. A word you cannot say is a word you will avoid.

## Measuring progress

A vocabulary test is a sample, so a change of one or two questions does not mean much. Look for a pattern: are you answering more questions correctly in the higher bands, and can you now explain why the wrong choices are wrong? Also keep a list of words that you have used in your own writing or speaking, because that is the most honest measure.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many new words should I learn per day?

Five to ten is a workable range for most adult learners, but the right number is the one you can review properly. Start with three to five if you have little time.

### Is it better to learn word lists or words from reading?

Words from your own reading are easier to remember because you meet them in context and in topics you care about. A word list can help for a specific exam, if you also practice using the words in sentences.

### How do I stop forgetting words?

Review each word on a schedule (after about a day, a week and a month), and use it in your own sentences. Words that you use stay; words that you only read tend to fade.

### Should I translate new words into my first language?

A translation can help at first. Over time, try to add a short English meaning and an example sentence, because that moves you away from translating and toward thinking in English.
