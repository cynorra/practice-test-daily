<div class="key-takeaway"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> To learn GRE-level vocabulary, study a limited number of words well rather than a long list badly: make flashcards with a sentence, a short meaning and a root or word family, review them on a schedule, and read challenging articles so that you meet the same words in context. Test yourself on meaning in a sentence, not only on definitions.</p></div>

Search for "GRE vocabulary" and you will find lists of hundreds or thousands of words. The problem with a long list is that you cannot hold it in memory without a system, and a word you memorized as a one-word definition is hard to use when it appears in a sentence. This guide gives you a system that works for advanced vocabulary: how to choose words, how to build flashcards that help, and how to review.

We have not verified what the GRE currently tests, so check ETS's official GRE pages for the current format before you plan your preparation. The method below works for any advanced vocabulary, whether you are preparing for a graduate exam, a university course or professional reading.

## Choose words you will meet again

A word is worth learning if you have met it more than once. Good sources are:

- long-form articles in newspapers and magazines,
- academic abstracts and essays in your field,
- books you want to read anyway.

Keep a note on your phone and add words that you did not know but that you met in a clear sentence. Aim for words that appear in several different sources rather than rare words that appear once.

## Build flashcards that work

A card with a single word on the front and a one-word synonym on the back is the weakest design. Use this layout instead:

<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead><tr><th>Front</th><th>Back</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>The sentence where you met the word, with the word hidden or underlined</td><td>The word; a short meaning in plain English; part of speech; one more example sentence you wrote; the root or word family</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>

Paper cards or an app both work. What matters is that you make the cards yourself, because writing the sentence is part of the learning, and that you review them on a schedule.

## A review schedule

Review each card after about one day, one week, one month and three months. Move a card out of the daily stack once you can answer it correctly three times in a row, and move it back if you miss it. Short daily sessions of 15 to 20 minutes are easier to keep up than a weekly marathon.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Memorizing a list from A to Z.** Alphabetical lists put unrelated words next to each other and give no context.
2. **Learning only a translation or a single synonym.** Words have several senses, and the sentence shows which one applies.
3. **Skipping review.** A card you never review is a card you will forget.
4. **Learning words you will never meet.** Choose words from material you actually read.
5. **Ignoring pronunciation.** Say each word aloud so that you recognize it in spoken English too.

## How to test yourself

Use questions that show a word in a sentence and ask for the meaning in context, as our vocabulary test does. Then try the reverse: cover the meaning, read the sentence and say the word. Finally, write your own sentence and check that the word fits there.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many GRE vocabulary words should I learn?

There is no fixed number. A smaller list that you know deeply is more useful than a long list you only half-recognize, so start with the words you meet in your own reading.

### Are GRE vocabulary flashcards worth making?

Yes, if you make them yourself, put each word in a sentence and review them on a schedule. Ready-made decks can help, but they work best when you add your own sentences.

### Is it better to learn roots or whole words?

Both help. Roots let you guess unfamiliar words, and whole words with example sentences teach you how to use them, so combine them on the same card.

### How long before the test should I start?

Start as early as you can, because vocabulary grows slowly through repeated exposure. A few weeks of daily study is a reasonable minimum, and a longer period gives you more time to review.
