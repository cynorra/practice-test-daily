A full SAT practice test tells you two things a single worksheet can't: where your score stands today, and which skills cost you the most points. But it only tells you that if you take it the way the real test runs. A practice exam done in pieces, with your phone open and no clock, mostly measures how well you do with unlimited time.

This guide covers how to set up a realistic practice test at home, how to pace each module, and the part most students skip: what to do with the results.

## Where to find a full-length SAT practice test

The Digital SAT is adaptive: the second module of each section changes based on how you did on the first. A realistic full-length practice test has to copy that, and College Board's Bluebook app does. It's the same app used on test day, and it includes full-length adaptive practice tests with the built-in Desmos graphing calculator and reference sheet. If you want a score you can trust, take your full practice tests there.

What you won't get from a full test is targeted repetition. A full SAT practice exam gives you only a handful of questions on any one skill. That's where skill-by-skill practice comes in, and it's what this site is built for:

- [SAT Math practice](/sat/math/) by skill, with unlimited new question sets
- [SAT Reading and Writing practice](/sat/reading-writing/) by skill, with an explanation for every answer choice

A good rhythm is one full practice test every week or two, with skill practice on the days in between.

## Set up the test like test day

Treat the practice test as a dress rehearsal. These details matter more than they sound:

1. **Block out about two and a half hours.** The test itself takes about 2 hours and 14 minutes, plus the break and setup.
2. **Use the device you'll test on.** Scrolling, highlighting, and using the on-screen calculator feel different on a laptop than on a tablet.
3. **Start at the time of day your real test starts.** If your test begins in the morning, take practice tests in the morning.
4. **Phone in another room.** Not face down on the desk. In another room.
5. **No pausing.** If you stop the clock to check an answer or take a call, the result no longer predicts anything.
6. **Have scratch paper and a pencil.** You'll want them for math setups and for marking questions to revisit.

The per-question times are averages, not targets for every item. A short punctuation question in Reading and Writing can take 30 seconds; a two-sentence synthesis question can take two minutes. In Math, a one-step linear equation should go fast so you have time for the multistep word problem near the end.

A simple checkpoint system keeps you honest without staring at the clock:

- **Reading and Writing:** about 14 questions done when 16 minutes remain.
- **Math:** about 11 questions done when 17 to 18 minutes remain.

If you're behind at the checkpoint, stop rereading passages and start trusting your first careful read. For a fuller breakdown, see the guide to the [Digital SAT format and timing](/guides/digital-sat-format/).

## What to do during the test

**Answer every question.** There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so a blank is always worse than a guess. If you're stuck, eliminate what you can, pick from what's left, flag the question, and move on.

**Use the flag tool.** Each module lets you mark questions and return to them before time runs out. Flag anything that took you more than about twice the average time, then come back if you finish early.

**Don't try to read the adaptive module.** If the second module feels harder, that's usually a good sign, not a reason to panic. Keep working each question on its own terms.

**Use Desmos for what it's good at.** Graphing both sides of an equation, finding where two lines cross, or checking a quadratic's roots can be faster on the calculator. Simple arithmetic usually isn't.

## Grade the test the useful way

Your score is one number. Your missed questions are the actual information. Right after the test, while you still remember your thinking, go through every question you got wrong *and* every question you guessed on, even if the guess was right.

For each one, write down three things:

1. **The skill.** For example, "percent change," "comma before a conjunction," or "transition words."
2. **What happened.** Didn't know the rule, misread the question, made an arithmetic slip, or ran out of time.
3. **The fix.** The rule or step that would have gotten it right, in one sentence.

After one practice test, you'll usually see two or three skills that account for a large share of your misses. Those are your study priorities until the next full test.

## Turn the results into practice

Say your error log shows misses on percent change, systems of equations, and comma and semicolon questions. Your next week might look like this:

- Two sessions of [percentages practice](/sat/math/percentages/), reading every explanation, including on the questions you get right
- Two sessions of [systems of equations](/sat/math/systems-of-linear-equations/) practice, solving each one two ways
- One session of [boundaries](/sat/reading-writing/boundaries/) (punctuation between clauses), with the rule written out before you start
- A short mixed set at the end of the week to see if the fixes stuck

Then take another full practice test and compare the error logs, not just the scores. Fewer misses in your target skills means the practice is working, even if the total score moves slowly at first.

## Common mistakes with SAT practice tests

**Taking test after test without review.** Five full practice tests with no error log teach you less than two tests with a careful review of each.

**Retaking the same test too soon.** You'll remember answers, and the score will look better than it should.

**Practicing only your weak section.** It makes sense to focus there, but keep a little practice in your strong section so it doesn't slip.

**Judging progress by a single score.** Scores from one practice test to the next bounce around. Look at the trend across several tests and at whether your error log is shrinking.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long is an SAT practice test?

A full-length Digital SAT practice test takes about 2 hours and 14 minutes of testing time, plus a short break between the Reading and Writing section and the Math section. Plan for about two and a half hours in total.

### How many SAT practice tests should I take?

Most students do well with one full practice test every week or two, with skill practice in between. What matters most is reviewing each test carefully; an extra test without review adds little.

### Is this site's practice the same as an official SAT practice test?

No. This site is independent and not affiliated with College Board. It offers skill-by-skill practice questions written in the style of the SAT. For full-length adaptive practice tests, use College Board's Bluebook app.

### Can I take an SAT practice test online for free?

Yes. Bluebook's full-length practice tests are free, and the skill practice on this site is free with no account needed.

### Should I guess on questions I don't know?

Yes. The SAT does not take points off for wrong answers, so eliminate the choices you can rule out, pick one of the rest, and flag the question if you want to come back to it.
